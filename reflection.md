# Reflection: Choosing What Remains Mine

Ten years from now, if everyone has an AI agent that can write, program, research, and manage complicated decisions, I would want to be someone whose judgment and commitments people can trust. I would still welcome useful tools and care about doing good work. However, I would not want the speed of my output to become the main explanation of who I am. My identity would depend on what I choose to pursue, how I treat people affected by those choices, and whether I accept responsibility when the results disappoint me.

The ambitions I would choose to preserve are building a meaningful career, achieving enough financial independence to make principled decisions, and understanding difficult problems. Their meaning would change. A prestigious title would matter less if an agent could perform most of its associated tasks. I would measure a career partly by whether the work serves a need I can explain and defend. Financial independence would be a source of freedom to refuse work I consider harmful. Learning would remain valuable because I want to understand the decisions I endorse, even when a machine can produce a more sophisticated answer.

Beyond productivity and professional achievement, I would want to be known as someone who pays attention and follows through. A friend should be able to share uncertainty without receiving an immediate attempt to optimise their life. A teammate should know that I will acknowledge an error rather than hide behind an impressive report. These qualities would show up in ordinary actions: keeping promises, giving credit, listening before offering advice, and remaining available when helping is inconvenient. An agent might remind me to do these things, but the relationship would still depend on my willingness to make time for another person.

I would also remain willing to struggle for understanding, physical health, and relationships. I would sometimes solve a problem before asking an agent for its answer, because that effort reveals which assumptions I have actually understood. I would exercise even if technology could make everyday life effortless, because living in a body involves capacities I want to develop through practice. I would have difficult conversations myself instead of outsourcing every message. I would not preserve hardship simply to prove that I can suffer. I would preserve efforts that build a capacity or a connection I consider part of a worthwhile life.

The skills I would prioritise are asking clear questions, checking evidence, recognising uncertainty, and making decisions when values conflict. An AI system might estimate the consequences of a financial decision, but someone must still decide whose interests count and what risks are acceptable. I would want enough technical and financial literacy to challenge an attractive recommendation and explain its limits. Patience, intellectual honesty, and the courage to disagree would matter alongside those skills. Experiences involving collaboration, service, and contact with people outside my usual environment would help me notice consequences that a polished answer might leave invisible.

If my value were no longer determined mainly by what I can do, I would define myself through commitments that remain meaningful without public recognition. Am I fair when a shortcut would benefit me? Do I change my mind when the evidence changes? Can people rely on me when there is no reward? I would resist turning these questions into another performance score. They would be standards for reflection and correction. My value as a person would not need to rise and fall with my usefulness, although I would still have a responsibility to contribute where I can.

Tomorrow, I would begin with small practices. Before using an agent for an important task, I would write down the purpose, the people affected, and the decision I must own. I would reserve regular time for learning without immediate assistance, maintain an exercise routine, and give people periods of undivided attention. For consequential recommendations, I would keep a brief decision record of the evidence, uncertainty, and reasons for acting. The future I want would therefore start with a practical boundary: delegate work thoughtfully while continuing to practise judgment, care, and responsibility myself.
